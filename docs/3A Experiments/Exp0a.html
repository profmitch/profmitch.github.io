Experiment 0a: Lab Exploration

Here is the lab manual excerpt for this experiment (Lab 0a).

Purpose of Experiment

Quite a few departmental instructors skip this lab experiment but I like it since it throws students in the deep end of the pool with the use common instruments like the buret (burr ette, the "t" not silent), the volumetric pipet, and the volumetric flask as analytical instruments. You also use a ring stand with clamp for the buret, and beakers and perhaps graduated cylinder as preparative equipment and not analytical equipment.

Buret

The buret is designed for the technique of titration, where an unknown variable amount of liquid is to be delivered and its volume to be used quantitatively to deliver a known amount of substance, given the concentration is known as well. We use the 25 mL size buret. The major (numbered) divisions on the buret are 1 mL, starting with 0 at the top, then 1, 2, 3, ... 25 to the bottom of the buret. The design of any experiment using a buret must keep the volume delivered between 0 and 25 mL, although the final volume target will be less than 25 mL.

Each division mark in the buret is 0.1 mL, which makes the buret precise to 0.01 mL.

The initial setup of a buret requires a clean buret. If droplets are seen sticking to the glass sides within the lumen, then it needs cleaning. Use a small amount of (dishwashing) detergent in water, fill it up maybe 10-20%, then invert the buret several times tip up and tip down with stopcock fully open to ensure the bore of the buret (the part below the stopcock) gets clean as well. Then remove the detergent solution and add deionized/distilled water about 10-20%, and rinse using the repeated inversion. This rinsing may need to be repeated several times with fresh water so that no foaming or detergent bubbles are seen.

With a fully cleaned and rinsed buret, now add to 10-20% of buret volume the solution that is to be used as titrant. This is a pre-rinse of the buret with the solution to be used so that it is not diluted by rinse water. This can be done once or twice if the stock solution is not too costy. Mount the buret into the clamp. Ensure that the buret is near to perfectly vertical: look at it as it would be read, then look at it from a 90-degree angle. That ensures verticality. The final step is to open the stopcock to flush out the bore of the buret, as it usually has air bubbles. The buret is ready for use.

Volumetric (Transfer) Pipet

These have a narrow tubular appearance, some of them with an enlargement of the middle of the tube's diameter. One end (the top) has a place to place a device (usually a pipet bulb) that will draw and release the fluid to be drawn into the pipet. The other end is the tip that is placed into the fluid used as the stock which will be drawn into the pipet. The tip has an narrowing of the pipet glass body which will make contact with the container to which the fluid will be delivered.

The various capacities of these pipets is given below, with the usual bold underlined. The 10 mL pipet is precise to 10.00 mL

  • Micro/Small Capacities: 0.5 mL, 1 mL, 2 mL, 3 mL, 4 mL, 5 mL
  • Medium/Standard Capacities: 10 mL, 15 mL, 20 mL, 25 mL
  • Large Capacities: 50 mL, 100 mL

Volumetric Flask

These have a distinct shape with a pear-like bottom and a long narrow tube-like neck. The neck contains a marking where the fluid level must be brought to which is the final volume of the flask.

The mixing of the components added to the flask occurs in two steps. First, if adding any solid (salt) to be dissolved, the solvent must be added sufficient to dissolve the solid but do not bring it fully to the beginning of the neck, but rather a centimeter or more below the neck. This allows mixing by swirling of the flask until the solid completely dissolves. Alternatively dissolve the solid in an appropiately small beaker first, add the solution to the volumetric flask, and then wash in several small aliquots of solvent in the beaker to ensure quantitative transfer.

Once the first phase of mixing is done, then add solvent to the mark in the flask neck, ensuring the meniscus bottom is on the mark line. Close the flask with stopper or parafilm securely, then invert the flask 10-20 times (do it 17 times, something believed to be optimal). The solution is ready in the volumetric flask.

Volumetric flask sizes can have a large range. Usual capacities are in bold underlined in the list below. The precision of a 50 mL flask will be considered 50.00 mL. The tolerances of a flask like the 50 mL come in two classes. Class A is high precision typically in a professional analytical chemistry lab. It will be 50.00 ± 0.05 mL. Class B is typical for general purpose or educational lab and is 50.00 ± 0.10 mL

  • Micro/Small Capacities: 1 mL, 2 mL, 5 mL, 10 mL, 20 mL, 25 mL
  • Medium/Standard Capacities: 50 mL, 100 mL, 200 mL, 250 mL, 500 mL
  • Large Capacities: 1000 mL (1 L), 2000 mL (2 L), 5000 mL (5 L)

Beakers and Erlenmeyer flasks.

Although these containers have volume markings on the side, they are never accurate enough for analytical determinations of volume. They are only estimated volumes to be used in preparative work. The only volumetric containers ever to be used to determine accurate volumes are graduated cylinders, volumetric flasks, volumetric pipets, and burets, to name the most common ever to be encountered. Never use beakers or Erlenmeyer flasks to report volumes you want to be accurate.

Techniques Used

Pipetting

Using a pipet bulb.The instructor will demonstrate how to use the pipet bulb. The pipet bulb has three squeeze points that are actually labeled: Air, S, and E.

The A or Air squeeze point is squeezed while you squeeze the bulb itself as much as possible to remove all the air in it. This will create the vacuum that will draw up the fluid into the pipet. You may find yourself squeezing the bulb a coupple of times in order to fully draw up liquid into the pipet, often depending on the capacity of the pipet.

This YouTube video demonstrating numerous aspects and pointing out safe use of the pipet in technique is excellent.

Titration With The Buret

Bring the titrant to a level below the "0" mark but slightly below it. (Between the 0 and 3 mL marks is typical.) Take the initial reading: make sure the bottom of the mensicus is read. The precision of the reading is "X.00 mL" (two decimal places). Turn the stopcock to deliver fluid. Fully opening it can deliver a continuous stream, which would be usual when this is to be done initially and it is known that a large amount is to be delivered. The stopcock is to be turned to being partially closed however then the delivery is to be drop-by-drop. It can even be adjusted to that a drop rate of one per second, one every two seconds, and even slower when necessary.

In delivering the fluid from the buret there is a simultaneous mixing of the container receiving the fluid. This is often an Erlenmeyer flask with the analyte fluid. The Erlenmeyer has the nice geometry in that it has a narrow mouth so that swirled fluid has less chance of drop escaping the container, and it has a wide bottom to achieve efficient rapid mixing by the swirling action. Try to avoid drops or delivered fluid dropping on to the side of the flask as it takes some time for the titrant to fall down the side. Try to ensure the fluid from the buret drops directly into the fluid in the flask.

When doing an acid-base titration, an indicator dye like phenolphthalein is added to the analyte solution. Phenolphthalein is usually added to a colorless solution that is on the acidic side. The titrant is a stong base like NaOH (sodium hydroxide), and as it falls from the buret to the analyte solution, the drops have a dark pinkish red color. If swirled and the color disappears, the endpoint is not yet reached. Make sure the delivery is drop by drop and slow. When a pinkish color that is very faint but detectable that persists with swirling, the endpoint is reached.
